    Circuit-level equivariance is code-dependent: obstruction in the surface code, exactness in the honeycomb Floquet code, and consequences for neural decoders

    Whether exact (twisted) equivariance survives at circuit level depends on the code. It is obstructed for surface-code extraction circuits and exact for the honeycomb Floquet code, whose C3 rotation mixes the logical qubits. Tying that symmetry into the decoder flips the honeycomb task from unlearnable to learned.

    The lattice’s teeth: primitive reciprocal axes govern which Fourier components neural cellular automata can grow

    Neural cellular automata are trained on square grids by default; this paper treats the lattice as an experimental variable. An apparent advantage of hexagonal over square grids turned out to be a normalisation artefact in cross-lattice comparisons, and the paper documents and retracts it. The second result was put to a pre-registered, out-of-sample test, and the paper reports the outcome.

Note 01Quantum hardware · 3 devices

Anyon braiding on three quantum computers

Topological order on the Kitaev honeycomb, measured on superconducting hardware with a matched-depth control beside every claim. Fluxes stayed conserved (+0.64) while a control destroyed them (+0.12). A single anyon flipped its plaquette to −0.905 ± 0.010 while the neighbour sat at +0.924 ± 0.015.

ResultThe −1 braiding statistics (Fig. 1), reproduced on ibm_marrakesh, ibm_kingston and ibm_fez with error bars. Not new physics: measured by one person, on three independent machines.

Note 02Quantum error correction · related: paper [1]

Symmetry-tied neural decoders for quantum error correction

A decoder reads a quantum machine’s error alarms and has to name the fault. Labelled data from real devices is scarce, so the right inductive bias matters more than raw scale.

ResultAt identical parameter count, the symmetry-tied decoder wins at every training size, is ~4× more sample-efficient, and reaches the matching-decoder baseline (0.9943 vs 0.9948). On the honeycomb Floquet code, the identical plain twin never learns the task the symmetry-aware one solves.

About

Independent research, Auckland.

I’m Daniel Olliver, in Auckland. Through 2025 and 2026 I’ve done independent research in geometric machine learning and quantum computing: more than two dozen controlled experiments on a GTX 1070, free cloud GPUs and three of IBM’s quantum computers.

“No result gets written down before its run completes.”The one rule, from the essay

Early on I caught myself writing numbers for an experiment that was still running. When it finished, reality was backwards from what I’d written. I corrected it and logged the incident: conviction will fabricate evidence if you let it.

The idea I set out to prove, that hexagonal wiring helps a network learn, came back cleanly false. What survived is narrower and more useful: symmetry helps a network when it is exactly present and data is scarce, and topology shows up on real quantum hardware.
